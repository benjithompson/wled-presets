# WLED Presets

A web page where anyone on your network can control all your WLED lights at once, and an admin console to set it up.

- **Public page** — tap a **scene** to play a preset on every device, or pick a solid color. Below the scenes, the **games** have their own group of buttons the same size: one tap opens the game on the Play page and starts it. Live status shows which lights are online. The **roofline** glows from the whole border of the window: the real roof lights as they are right now, in strip order, wrapped clockwise from the bottom-left corner (the roof's left end up the left side, its right end down the right). Its light pours in from the edges into the animated background. It shows the frames the server sends the roof (effects, games), and for a roof device that plays its own preset or color, what WLED reports over its WebSocket (the same live view as "Peek" in the WLED app). When you are signed in as an admin, a bar on the page links back to the admin console and has the power switch and brightness for every light. Light and dark themes. Works well on phones and can be added to the home screen.
- **Admin console** (`/admin`)
  - **Scenes** — create, edit, reorder, test, export and import. For each device, a scene picks either one of its **WLED device presets** or one of the **built-in effects** (drawn by the server on all that device's lights, with its colors, speed and brightness under **Colors, speed…**), with no trip to the layout editor. Built-in effects need the device's lights in the layout. "Quick fill" sets every device to a preset with the same name, or every device to the same built-in effect. For effects on single objects or segments, use the layout editor (see below; **Effects per object** on the Scenes page): an object's own effect wins over its device's.
  - **Effects** — forty-three effects that the server draws for each LED, 60 times a second, in floating-point color, and streams to the lights. Thirteen are **basics**, the standard ones you would expect: Solid color (one color, or the string split into equal parts), Gradient, Color blocks (red, green, red, green…), Rainbow, Color fade, Breathe, Blink, Strobe, Theater chase, Color wipe, Twinkle, Sparkle and Bouncing dot; with **My colors** they play exactly the colors you pick, and controls that do nothing for an effect (the speed of a solid color) are hidden. Ten run along one object's LEDs as a string (1-D), from its first point to its last (or back), skipping off segments: Aurora, Fireworks (rockets, bursts and sparks with drag and crackle), Inferno, Meteor shower, Ocean (waves of several lengths with foam on the crests), Plasma, Starlight (with shooting stars), Lightning storm, Raindrops (ripples that spread and cross) and Candy cane. Twenty are 3-D: they light each LED by where it is in the yard, so they fill a bush, spiral up a tree, or play over the whole yard as one picture: Color wash, Laser scan, Bonfire (fire that climbs from the ground), Vortex, Shockwaves, Nebula, Snowfall (flakes that leave a glow where they touch), Fireflies, Starburst (3-D fireworks), Equalizer, and ten big shows for the whole yard: Hyperspace (stars stream past, stretch into streaks and jump with a flash), Digital rain, Plasma globe (crackling bolts from a glowing core), Black hole (a swirling disk of fire, a photon ring and jets), Lava lamp (blobs that melt into each other), Double helix, Hologram (a turning wireframe cube, diamond and 4-D tesseract), Orbits (comets round a nucleus), Supernova (collapse, flash, shock wave, afterglow and a pulsar) and Kaleidoscope. 3-D effects have a **Direction** (left to right, upward, toward the street, from the middle out…). The 3-D pictures (all but the washes, scans, nebula and shockwaves, which already go every way) also have a **View**: from the street, facing the house, or from above, lying flat over the yard, so a vortex or a kaleidoscope spreads over the lawn and the lights on the ground. With nothing selected in the layout editor, **Whole yard effect** gives every object an effect at once (a 3-D one as one picture over the yard, a 1-D one along each object); objects and segments with their own effect play theirs instead. A single segment of a roof line or other line can play its own effect: click the segment, then pick one in its **Segment effect** section; the line's effect fills the rest. To give several objects and segments one effect, Ctrl/⌘-click them (in the views, the object list or the 3D view; Shift-click in the list picks a range; Esc clears): the inspector then edits them together, and a setting you change goes to each of them. In the layout editor, pick an object's effect, colors (19 palettes or your own), speed, intensity, size and brightness in its **Effect** section; **Play effects** (P) animates them in every view and, with Lights on, on the real lights. **Scenes ▾** saves them as a scene for the public page, or opens a scene to change it (the Scenes page links there too). A playing scene keeps running with nobody watching, shows in the Play page's simulator, pauses for a game and comes back after it, and plays again after a restart. Choosing another scene or a color stops it.
  - **Sequences** — an editor for animated light shows, built like a video editor (Admin → Sequences). A preview of the yard (front or 3D) plays exactly what the lights get, with a **Now playing** list of every clip and transition at the playhead; point at an object to see what lights it. Below it, a timeline with a ruler, a playhead, markers and **layers**: drag an effect from the browser onto a layer (or double-click a layer) to add a **clip**, which plays that effect on the objects, segments or whole yard you picked (in the Objects tab or by clicking the preview). Drag clips to move them (also to another layer; Alt-drag copies), drag their edges to trim, and drag the knobs at their top corners to fade in and out. Upper layers cover lower ones, mixed by each layer's blend mode (Normal, Add, Lighten, Multiply) and opacity; layers can be hidden, solo or locked, and reordered by their grip. Two clips that overlap on a layer **cross over**: one effect into the next, or one object to another; drop a **transition** on a clip to choose how (Crossfade, Dip to black, Wipe in four directions, Iris, Sparkle dissolve). **Audio tracks** take MP3, WAV, OGG, M4A or FLAC files (uploaded to the server, shown as waveforms, trimmable) and play in the browser in step with the lights; set a tempo to see beats and snap to them, or press M while it plays to tap markers. Snapping (clip edges, markers, the playhead, beats), zoom (Ctrl/⌘ + wheel), a box to select, split (S), copy and paste, duplicate, undo and Ctrl/⌘+S work as in other editors. **Lights** plays it on the real lights as you edit. **Use in a scene** makes a scene that plays the sequence (it loops) for the public page; a scene's editor can also pick a sequence. **New showcase** (in the sequence menu) builds a 60-second show at 128 BPM for your own yard, with music made to match, through an intro, a build, a drop, a breakdown, a second drop and a finale. The trees play the kick drum, the bushes the hi-hats and the bass, the roof's segments the melody (low notes on the left, high ones on the right), and an arpeggio hops from object to object. It is mostly motion on dark: every quarter note moves somewhere, a different way every two bars, and what moves grows with the music. A light walks along the roof one key a beat in the intro; the bushes wipe side to side, then a laser scans across them a pass a beat in the build; each bush wipes up and down, then a laser scans up the whole ground in the rise; in the drops a laser crosses the ground, every object wipes side to side or up and down on its own, waves cross the yard, and a band of a new color runs along the roof on every beat; the breakdown goes nearly dark, one object a beat; the second drop moves everything at once, with lasers across the yard and up the trees. Each chord brings its own color and accent, so the colors change every bar and every beat. The yard's 3-D pictures (hyperspace, an equalizer, snowfall, a kaleidoscope, a hologram, fireworks and a supernova) play only where the music swells, under half of the show. A clip's **Each object** switch (in its Transitions) makes its wipes cross every object on its own instead of all of them as one. The music is synthesized in the browser and uploaded as its audio track; edit the show like any other sequence, and save it to keep it. **Save to file** (also in the sequence menu) downloads the sequence as it is in the editor, saved or not, as a `.wledseq` file with its music inside; **Open from file** opens one as a new sequence on this site or another (its music is uploaded; save it to keep it). Clips name the objects they light by their ids in the layout, so on another yard the clips for objects it doesn't have light nothing until you retarget them.
  - **Devices** — add by address or scan the network. The presets stored on a device are imported when you add it, and again when its address changes; **Import** reads them again at any time. Play a preset to test it, and enable or disable a device. **Lights** at the top switches every light (or one device) on or off and sets the brightness. Off holds: scenes, colors, games and the auto-revert leave a switched-off light dark until you switch it on, and with every light off, visitors cannot change them. The editors' own switches for the lights (the layout editor's **Lights**, the Games page's **Show on the lights**, a custom object's **Light up now**, the sequence editor's preview) still light a device that is off, here or in WLED itself, while you use them; it goes off again after. Brightness for all lights (or a device's own level) is put back after each scene, so presets with their own brightness follow it; **Let scenes decide** hands brightness back to the presets. Each device shows what it reports itself, and the app follows changes made on the devices: switch a light off or dim it in WLED's own app, with its button or with one of its timers, and the switch here moves too (the Activity log says "outside this app"). Every enabled device keeps one WebSocket open to the server for this (the home page's roofline borrows it), and the status check reads the state as well, for a device whose WebSocket does not work. A device that does not answer shows as offline, not as on or off; a switch made while it was offline goes to it when it answers again, so lights switched off stay off after a power cut. **WLED settings** (the sliders button on a device, or its ⋯ menu on a phone) changes the device's own settings without WLED's app: its LED outputs (LED type, GPIO pins, first LED and count, color order, reversed, LEDs to skip, current per LED), the brightness limiter (off, one limit for the device, or one on each output), what it does at power-up (on or off, brightness, preset), brightness factor, transition and gamma, how it takes real-time frames (on or off, timeout, full brightness, gamma, LED offset) and WLED's own sync, its names and Wi-Fi radio (sleep, transmit power), and everything else as JSON. The LED types and the GPIO pins the board cannot use come from the device itself. Save sends only what changed, and says what the device did not take. Wi-Fi networks, the device's access point and its update lock are not changed from here (a mistake there would cut the device off); a device with a settings PIN can be read but not changed. **Restart** restarts it.
  - **Layout** — place every light object where it really is, in 3D: rooflines, other lines of lights (windows, doors, a fence, a path), bushes and trees (a spiral, or strands that zigzag). The front view (over a picture of the house) moves objects across and up, the top view across and toward the street, and the 3D view (three.js, loaded only when opened) shows the whole yard with glowing LEDs; click an LED there to select its object. The roofline sets the real scale (feet or meters): its LEDs are a known distance apart on their strings, so from corner to corner (as matched to the lights) it is as long as its string, and the top view, the 3D view and the picture from above follow; more strings or a corner's LED moved change the scale, and the objects keep the size they are drawn at. Without a roof with a light string, or to set it by hand, a measure tool (or the front view's width) sets it; **Use the roofline** in **Yard size** goes back. A ground line sets height 0, and the side panel takes exact positions. The editor is a CAD-style workspace that fills the window: a tool palette (V select, A edit points, C split, H pan, R roofline, L line, B bush, T tree, M measure), one to three viewports (each one shows the front, top or 3D view and can be made big), an object list in which the selected object opens to show its properties right under it (a click on its header again closes it and selects nothing), and a status bar with the pointer position and the Snap, Ortho, Grid and Grid snap switches. The grid size next to them opens the grid settings: the size and its units (feet, inches, meters or centimeters, whatever the units of the layout), quick sizes, and how often a brighter line comes; these are remembered in the browser. The wheel zooms at the pointer, the middle button or Space-drag pans, F zooms to the selection, Home zooms to fit. Lines snap to points, midpoints, lines and alignments, and to the grid with Grid snap on (Alt holds snapping off); Shift keeps 45° steps while drawing, and Shift-drag moves a point or an object straight across or straight up and down from where it was (for example, to raise the middle of a roofline into a peak); a click on the end of a line continues it; Ctrl/⌘+Z undoes; Ctrl/⌘+S saves. **File → Export the layout** downloads it as one JSON file (as it is in the editor, unsaved changes too) with its pictures, the WLED devices its objects use and the game area; **File → Import a layout** puts such a file (or a bare layout) in place of the saved layout and its pictures, after asking. Objects keep their device when it is on this install (the same id, address or name); others are left without one. LEDs spread along the real 3D length of a line, so a roofline that runs back along the side of the house gets its share. Each object has the number of LEDs in it. Games and the simulator use this map. Each object has a WLED device and the LED numbers at its first and last point; LEDs between objects (gaps, a hidden side of the house) stay off. A roof line can be split at a corner, and a corner can be pinned to its real LED number. **Match to the lights** (in the line's **LEDs** section) does that for the whole line: draw its rough shape, then the wizard walks the start, each corner and the end, and one LED blinks red on the house; move it to the real spot (buttons, a slider, or the arrow keys, Shift for 10 at a time) and press **Found it** (Enter). The stretch from the point before up to the blinking LED glows, on the house and in the drawing, so it is easy to follow along the roof. The red LED never goes past the last LED the device has in WLED (its LED count, as set in WLED's LED preferences); at the end, **Last LED in WLED** jumps there for a line that ends the chain. When the line's lights come in strands (50 bulbs each, for example), the wizard says which strand and bulb the red LED is on, counted from LED 0 of the device, and Page Up/Page Down jump from one strand joint to the next. The start and the end can move past the line's LEDs, so the line also learns its real number of LEDs; a corner that lands past later corners unpins them, to be matched next. Typing the LED numbers of a line, a corner or a segment by hand is still there, folded under **Advanced**. **Shape to the lights** (in a tree's **LEDs** section, for a spiral or a back-and-forth wave) does the same for a tree: one LED blinks red on it, you move it along the string and say where you see it: where the string starts and ends (any of eight ways round, or the exact degrees), and each LED where it passes the front, the right, the back or the left (a wave: where it turns at the left or the right side, and how far round). Keys F, R, B, L mark it. Each mark has a height on the shape, set with − and + (or [ and ]) to pull the string up or down, and **Move it** moves the LED off the shape: left, right, up, down (Alt and the arrow keys) and toward the street or the house (Alt+Page Down, Alt+Page Up), 3 in (5 cm) at a time, so the cone, ball or cylinder only gives the first guess. The LEDs between two marks move along, by their share. A wave shaped this way is made of its rows: mark its first LED, each LED where the string turns at the left or the right, and its last LED. Each row is as wide as its LEDs go round (a row with fewer LEDs than the one below is narrower, so no cone or ball is needed), the widest row as wide as the tree, and that width comes from the light string whenever the marks change; the rows sit evenly apart up the tree's height (a turn's height can be set). **Width** and **Height** then stretch it by hand. On a spiral, the tree's pattern, bent to go through the marks, places the LEDs in between: it goes on round the way it winds, as many times as the pattern would between two marks, so a mark every quarter turn or only a few both work. Marked LEDs glow amber on the tree while you work; **Clear marks** puts the pattern back. Click one straight segment of a roof line to light up only that segment, set its start and end LED (segments never share an LED: when one segment ends at LED 49, the next starts at 50), or turn it off (skipped: for a stretch that only links two lit segments; its LEDs stay dark and games skip it). While you edit, the selected object glows blue on the simulator and the real lights (first LED green, last LED red, corners amber), and each change to its LED numbers shows at once through DDP over UDP (port 4048). The lights go back to normal about 2 seconds after you leave the editor. An object that is where it belongs can be **locked in place** (the lock next to its name when it is selected, its right-click menu, or **Lock all** for a selection): it can still be selected, and its LEDs, wiring and effect still change, but it cannot be dragged, resized, given or cut at a point, or deleted until it is unlocked. Locked objects show a lock in the object list, and the lock is saved with the layout. **Revert** still puts back the saved layout. A bush, a tree or a semicircle can become one of the others (**Kind** in its **Object** section, its right-click menu, or **Make them** for a selection, to turn every bush into a tree at once): it keeps its name, LEDs, light string, effect and the part of the yard it covers (as wide, on the same ground, as tall); the new kind's shape settings start at their defaults, and a bush becomes a ball-shaped tree. The selected object has handles on it in the front and top views, as in 3D programs: arrows move it along one axis (X across in red, Y up and down in green, Z toward the street in blue; the white square moves it freely), a ring turns it (the front view's ring turns a semicircle or custom object as seen from the front, the top view's ring turns which way a tree, semicircle or custom object faces; Shift for 15° steps), and yellow squares on its box resize its width, its height, or both together. A locked object has none. Trees have three LED patterns: a spiral, strands, or **back and forth (winding)**: level passes over the front of the tree, joined by round bends like a river's, instead of going all the way round. It has how far around the passes go (degrees, centered on the tree's front), how far apart they are, whether it starts at the base or the top, and which side (left or right) the first and the last LED are on. A ball-shaped tree has a **roundness** (its height as a share of its width: less is flatter) and how much of it has LEDs (**LEDs cover**, in degrees from the top: 90 is its top half, an upside-down bowl like the crown of a Japanese maple); a spiral on it winds in from the outside edge or out from the middle, either way round. A spiral's loops can be set by how far apart they are (**Loops apart**, measured over the tree's surface, which sets the turns) and can bunch toward the top or the middle of a ball's cap, or toward the base or its outside edge (**Loops bunch**); evenly, they sit the same distance apart over the surface. A fourth view, **Side** (from the left, the street on the right; pick it in any viewport's view menu), shows depth and height: objects move there along Z and Y, and its ring tilts a tree, semicircle or custom object toward the street or the house (**Leans to street**, or **Tilt**). The front view's ring leans a tree left or right (**Leans right**), about its base. New objects are drawn in the front or top view. On a touch screen the panel's fields and buttons are finger-sized (44 px), with − and + beside every number. On a phone, a tree's or semicircle's shape settings come in three sections (**Shape**, **LED pattern**, **Turn and tilt**) instead of one long one, and **Shape to the lights** keeps the blinking LED's buttons on top with the rest in tabs (**Mark it**, **Move it**, **Marks**) and opens the panel half way so the tree stays in sight. To leave the views as much room as a phone allows, the layout editor has no page header there: the menu button at the start of its command bar has the other pages, the public page, the theme and Sign out, and the tool's hint floats over the bottom of the view. A finger held still on an object for half a second selects it. The object designer gets buttons for what keys and double-clicks do on a computer: **Done** and undo a point while drawing, zoom in and out, and **Add a point after** and **Delete point** for the picked point (on a computer too).
  - **Light strings** — tell the editor what each object is made of: a light string or strip with its chip, voltage, LED spacing, LEDs per strand and current per LED. Common ones are built in (including NovaBright 12 V WS2811 bullet pixels at 4 in, 50 per strand), and you can add your own. The editor then shows a line's drawn length against the length of its string, offers **Use N LEDs** to fit the LEDs to the line, marks where each new strand starts (with its LED number), and estimates the power. A single segment shows how many LEDs fit it at that spacing; **Use N LEDs here** gives it that many and moves the LEDs after it along, as on the real string. Pick a light string for **New objects** (Yard panel) and lines you draw get the right number of LEDs, with each segment's LED count shown while you draw. The string also limits the drawing, so the bulbs sit as far apart as on the real string: a line, or a custom object, cannot be dragged or sized longer than its string reaches (the editor says so); a tree's turns, strands or passes, a semicircle's rows and a bush's spiral are worked out from the string (they show greyed out, and follow when the LED count or the size changes), and a bush with a ring is as big as its string goes round. A tree made too big for its string even then stops growing. **Spacing** under **Made of** shows how far apart the LEDs are as drawn against the string's spacing, in red when the wire is drawn longer than the string. An object is made of whole strings: **Strings** under **Made of** sets how many (its LED count follows), new lines get whole strings, and an object with part of a string says so and offers the whole number. Where each string starts and ends is set in the object's **Strings** check (below), for a string that was cut or hides a few LEDs.
  - **Custom objects** — the object designer (O, or **New custom object**) draws how a string is laid out on your own object: a star, a wreath, an arch, a window, a candy cane, or anything else, from a template or point by point. A design can have several strokes (the wire jumps between them without LEDs), closed or open; the LEDs spread along the wire at the string's spacing and keep fitting it as you change the drawing. LED 0, the last LED and each new strand are marked. **From a photo**: the designer lights the object's LEDs in a color that stands out (its first LED in another, the rest of the lights dark), you take a picture of it from the front (best in the dark), and the lit LEDs become dots in the order the wire runs, sized by the string's LED spacing. **Scan with the camera** (at the https:// address) gives the exact order: the LEDs go a dim white, then in a few frames each shows red, green or blue, one base-3 digit of its number plus a check digit, and the camera reads each dot's number, even where the wire crosses itself and the hand shakes a little. The LEDs are found where the frames differ, and each is read against its own white, so a lamp, a colored wall or the camera's color balance do not get in the way; lay the string out so the LEDs do not hide behind each other. The string's LED spacing is the most two LEDs can be apart (the wire between them can be slack, never longer), so it sizes the shape from the longer steps, catches misread numbers (an LED far from both of its neighbors), and keeps every scanned LED where it really is: a scanned stroke has a point per LED (**An LED per point**), and an LED that was not read sits between its neighbors. The colors also place each LED: in the first color frame three LEDs in a row are red, green and blue, so looking at one color at a time their glows stay apart even where the wire bunches; each LED is then centered on its own color in the frames where its neighbors show other colors, and an LED's reflection (same number, dimmer) loses to the LED. **Save the scan frames** keeps the camera's pictures of a scan that went wrong. **In 3D**: after a scan, **Scan from another side**: step about an eighth of the way round the object (or turn it that much) and scan again, as often as you like, all the way round for LEDs on every side. Every LED is where the lines of sight from the scans that saw it meet, as in motion capture; where each scan was taken from is worked out from the same LEDs (the way round, how far, the camera's tilt), so the steps need not be exact. The first scan is the front; the 3D view turns the result round, and **Swap front, back** fixes a shape that came out mirrored. **Walk round in AR** (Chrome on Android, with Google Play Services for AR): the lights blink the code over and over while you walk slowly round the object with the phone, in a lit room. The phone's AR tracking (WebXR, ARCore: its camera and motion sensors together) says where it is for every picture, in meters, so each LED is followed from picture to picture, read when it has gone through the whole code, and placed where its lines of sight meet: the shape comes out at its real size, with no steps to take. Green rings over the camera show the LEDs read so far; **Done** works out the shape. Placed in the yard, a 3D object faces the way you turn it, and 3-D effects light it by where its LEDs really are; the designer shows its front and keeps each point's depth (**Point depth**). When the order is wrong or the LEDs were not found, trace the wire by hand: drag through the dots, or along the wire, with the mouse or a finger (two fingers zoom). Dots can be added and removed, and the first LED picked. The photo stays in the browser. Designs go to a library: place one in the yard as often as you like, scale it, turn it and face it toward the street or to the side. Changing a design changes every copy.
  - **Checks against the real lights** — the layout editor can show, on the house and in the drawing at the same moment, where the drawing puts each LED, so you can see what is placed or wired wrong. With nothing selected, **Check alignment** (or the scan-line button in the tool palette) runs a **slice**: a thin plane of white light across the whole yard, along the width, the height or the depth. It sweeps by itself, or holds where you put it (type a distance, or drag the white line in the front or top view); LEDs on either side of it glow blue and orange. Where the white lands on the house should line up the way it does in the drawing: an object that lights early or late is drawn in the wrong place or at the wrong size. A **ruler** colors the yard in bands (red, green, blue, yellow, every 1–10 ft or 0.5–3 m) from the left edge, the ground or the front wall, so each real object should show the same colors as its drawing. For one object, **Check the wiring** has a **color test**: each LED colored by where the drawing puts it. It starts in two halves, one at a time: **Top · bottom**, **Left · right** or **Front · back** (the street side or the house side), the first half red and the second blue, LED 0 blinking white; halves an object is too thin to have (a long low roofline has no top and bottom) can't be picked. **All eight** shows every block at once: as seen from the street on the half toward the street (top left red, top right blue, bottom left yellow, bottom right green) and on the half toward the house (top left pink, top right cyan, bottom left white, bottom right orange), LED 0 blinking white. An object with no depth to speak of (a roofline along the front) shows only the four street-side colors; one that is long and low only left and right. The house shows the same clean blocks when the object's shape and wiring are right, and swapped or scrambled colors when they are not. A **chase** runs one light along the wire from LED 0. **Strings** lights each string of the object in a color of its own (red, green, blue, yellow, purple, cyan, then red again), its first LED blinking white and its last LED white, and lists each string's first and last LED (device LED numbers) to type where a real string starts or ends; the string next to it gets longer or shorter, and **Every 50 LEDs again** puts them back. With nothing selected, **Check alignment → Strings** does the same for every object at once. Arrows step through the ways the object's string can run (which end it starts at; for a tree, base up or top down and which way it winds; for a semicircle, rows or columns and from where; for a bush, a net, a ring or a spiral), skipping ways that would light the house the same, until the house matches.
  - **Pictures to trace over** — **Pictures ▾** in the layout editor takes a picture of the front of the house for the front view (a photo, or a Street View screenshot taken straight on) (taken from off to one side, from up high or from close by, set **Front view angle** with nothing selected, or the slider on the front view's title bar: the whole yard turns, and comes closer, under the picture until the objects line up with it; effects and games do not change) and a picture of the yard from above for the top view (a satellite view screenshot, for example from Google Maps, or a drone photo). The picture from above also lies on the ground in the 3D view. To line it up, **Align** asks for a spot on the picture (a front corner of the house), then where that spot really is (the end of the roofline; it snaps), then a second pair: the picture moves, turns and resizes to fit both (Enter after the first pair only moves it). Or measure something on it in the top view and type its real length, then **Scale the picture**. Its width, turn, position and strength (0 hides it) are in the inspector when nothing is selected. Then draw bushes and trees over it where they really are. Only signed-in users can load the picture from above, as it shows where the house is.
  - **Real lights** — a running game also goes to each object's WLED device; when the game ends (or the last Play page closes), each device goes straight back to its color, scene or preset. On the Play page, a signed-in admin can open **Debug** to turn this off or limit the brightness. The game goes to each object's WLED device (60 frames per second, DDP over UDP: long strips go in several packets and show as one frame; a device whose longest output cannot show 60 gets an even 30 or 20, which also spares a weak Wi-Fi link), with a brightness limit. Objects with no device stay in the simulator. Nothing is sent while no game runs. If DDP does not reach a device (for example, a router between two networks drops it), the app falls back to the WLED JSON API over HTTP at about 12 frames per second, and says so in the debug panel and the Activity log.
  - **Games** — which lights the games use. Games play the roof as it looks from the street, measured in the front view rather than in LEDs: a comet moving at a steady speed moves evenly across the front of the house, a stretch at an angle to the street is crossed faster (it looks shorter), and where the roof runs back along the side of the house or behind another part of the roof, so it cannot be seen from the street, games still light it but run through it very quickly (at most six times as many LEDs a second as along the front, so it shows as a quick streak and not one flash), with nothing to leave out (the picture shows these lights gray-blue, as **Not seen from the street**). This uses the depth of each point in the layout. Leave out other lights that cannot be seen (behind a tree): a whole object, single segments of a roof line or other line, or a number of LEDs at either end. A picture of the yard shows each part in the color of its job in games (roof, bushes, trees, the rest) and what is left out in red; point to an object or segment to find it, and **Show on the lights** shows the same picture on the house. Scenes and effects still use every light; lights left out stay dark during a game.
  - **Activity** — live log of what was applied, by whom, and every admin change.
  - **Settings** — page title and subtitle, color picker on or off, default scene and auto-revert time, discovery subnets, admin password, and **Passkeys**: sign in with a phone, computer or security key (fingerprint, face or PIN) instead of the password. **Add a passkey** asks for the password once, then the device makes the passkey; the sign-in page then has **Sign in with a passkey**, and browsers that can also offer it in the username field. Passkeys can be renamed or removed; a removed one stops working at once.

- **Taking turns** — the site can be open to anyone, so people take turns with the lights, like an arcade line or a light show's request jukebox. A tap when the lights are free plays at once. When they are busy, a scene, a color or a game joins **the line** (one place per visitor; a second tap changes what the place is for and keeps it), and both pages show what plays now, how many wait, your place and the longest you can wait for your turn (**Leave the line** gives it up). A scene or a color picked while nobody else waits keeps the lights only 10 seconds, so whoever comes along next (for a scene or a game) is not kept waiting for nothing; one whose turn comes while others still wait plays a minute before the next in line goes. It is also a throttle: after a scene or a color of theirs plays, a visitor cannot pick another for those 10 seconds, not even to change their own turn (the public page dims the buttons and counts down; signed-in admins are never throttled). Whoever has a scene on can switch it to a game at once while nobody else waits. A game opened from the public page while the lights are busy is only pointed out on the Play page: tapping it joins the line. A game turn lasts as long as the player likes while nobody waits; once someone does, it ends when the game ends (after the initials are typed) or five minutes after the wait began, whichever comes first. Only the player with the turn controls the game; everyone else watches the simulated yard (Roof Duel's second side stays open to anyone watching). When a game's turn comes, the next player's phone buzzes with **Your turn! PLAY**: they have 30 seconds to tap it, or the line moves on. A phone that loses its connection keeps its turn for 20 seconds, and its place in line until its turn comes; closing the page ends a turn at once. Visitors are told apart by a random id in a cookie, nothing else. Signed-in admins never wait: their taps take the lights at once. In **Admin → Games**, **Taking turns** shows the line, takes someone out of it or ends a turn, sets the times, or switches the line off (the last tap wins at once, fine on a home network).
- **Play** (`/play`) — games that use the lights on the house, controlled from a phone with one big button (tap it, or hold it down), or for some games a row of buttons (Bead Rush: change color left, fire, change color right; Beat Drop: one button per color), with keyboard keys on a computer (the space bar for the big button, the arrow keys for a row). While a game runs, it fits on one screen, on a phone or a computer: the **game window** (the yard like the screen of an arcade cabinet, zoomed in on the lights) takes all the room the message and the buttons leave. Its top bar shows the game's numbers (score, best, level, lives, stars, the team scores) and its bottom bar the progress, so nothing is drawn over the yard. The buttons are finger-sized on a touch screen and regular-sized for a mouse. The high scores are below the game, a scroll away. Some games have choices on their start screen (Beat Drop: 1, 2 or 4 colors, and the difficulty). **High scores**, arcade style: each scoring game keeps a table of its ten best scores with three-letter initials (Beat Drop one for each number of colors and difficulty), shown on its start and game-over screens. A score that makes the table goes on it straight away, and the phone asks for initials: ▲ and ▼ turn each letter, or type them on a computer (Enter saves; the phone remembers the last initials). It can be named until the next game starts, and stays as ??? otherwise. A short list of rude three-letter words is refused. The tables are kept in the database, so they last through restarts and updates; a signed-in admin sees an × on each line to take it off. (Comet Catcher and Roof Duel have no table: one is won by lighting every bush, the other is played between two players.) Every game uses the whole yard, and each part has the same job in every game: the **roof** is where the game is played; **bushes** hold what you have won; **trees** are meters that fill from the base up; **windows, doors and other lines** (and custom objects) are the house reacting: they catch the color of every explosion and glow warmer with a combo. Gold always means points, red a mistake. A yard without bushes, trees or windows still plays on the roof alone.
  - **Comet Catcher** — stop the comet inside the green gate on the roof; each catch lights a bush. The gate slowly closes in while the comet runs (to 40% of its width), opens up again after a miss, and each new gate starts a little narrower than the last.
  - **Cascade** — orbs in four colors (green, cyan, blue, magenta) roll along the roof under real gravity (they speed up down its slopes and bounce off each other). The bomb is the only white light on the roof: a comet with a burning fuse for a tail, the stretch its blast would cover lit faintly, and the orbs in it glowing brighter. One tap sets it off (when the fuse burns down after 12 seconds, it goes off by itself, blinking fast in the last 3); every orb it touches bursts and sets off its neighbours. A burst that pops an orb of its own color is a combo: double points and a bigger blast. Pop enough in one chain reaction to clear the level; pop every orb for a clean sweep (a bonus and a life back). Each popped orb drips its color off the gutter, past the windows, into the bush below; the trees fill like a thermometer. Later levels bring more orbs, smaller blasts, a gold orb (worth more, bigger blast) and armored orbs (with a shell of two dimmer dots) that need two blasts.
  - **Overflow** — hold the button to pour light from the gutter into each bush and tree in turn; let go so it settles inside the white ring. The pour speeds up the longer you hold, and the light keeps rising for a moment after you let go. Its color says how close you are: blue when far below, green and yellow nearer, gold in the ring, red over it. A stop in the ring fires a rocket up to the roof (dead center: PERFECT, a burst in every color and a shockwave over the house); successes in a row multiply the points. Later levels: a thinner ring, a faster pour, then a ring that moves.
  - **Roof Duel** — Fire (the left end of the roof) against Ice (the right end), on two phones; each phone turns its player's color. Tap when the ball is in your end zone to send it back: the later the hit, the faster it flies (a hit at the tip is a SMASH). The ball slows climbing to a peak and speeds up coming down. The roof is split between the teams and the line moves toward whoever is losing; each point flies into the scorer's bushes, the trees heat up with a long rally, and each side's windows glow in its team's color. First to 5. Alone, tap twice to play the house.
  - **Slice** — a band of light lies on the roof: the top layer of a tower. The next layer slides back and forth along the roof at the same width, with white edges (the only white on the roof). Tap to drop it: what lands on the band stays and becomes the new top layer, and whatever hangs over the edge breaks off, flashes red and falls from the gutter. So each layer is a little narrower, and the slider a little faster; miss the band entirely and the tower falls. A drop with the edges lined up is PERFECT: nothing breaks off and the band flashes gold. Five perfects in a row, and every perfect after that grows the band back a little, so a perfect player can stack forever. A clock runs down while the slider moves (8 seconds to start, 10 at most, shown on the phone), and every drop winds it back up, more for a perfect. What a drop gives back is measured in the time the slider takes to cross the roof: dropping on its first pass gains time, waiting for it to come round again loses it. In the last 3 seconds the roof and the windows tick red, faster in the last one and a half; when the clock runs out, the tower falls. The trees are the tower itself, one band of color per layer stacking up from the base (perfect layers sparkle gold); the bushes fill with the perfect streak, and all sparkle gold once the band grows back. The layers come round in green, cyan, blue and magenta.
  - **Color Gate** — your light sits near the start of the roof, and walls of green, blue and pink light stream toward it from the far end. Each tap changes your color, always in the same order: green, blue, pink. A wall that reaches you in your color passes through for points (gold sparks); one in another color shatters red and costs a life. The next wall to reach you has white brackets, the only white on the roof. A tap that comes a moment late over Wi-Fi still counts. There is no finish line: the walls come faster and closer together, then in quick runs that need two taps between them, up to a pace a sharp player can keep up forever. Walls in a row multiply the points, up to ×4. The bushes all glow your color, so you can read it from the street; the trees fill toward the next multiplier, and the windows catch each wall's color and glow warmer with the streak.
  - **Ricochet** — a white comet races along the roof toward a green gate. Tap while it is in the gate and it turns around at once, with the next gate already waiting somewhere ahead of it (sometimes past a bounce off the end of the roof). There is no pause between gates, the comet gets 2% faster with every catch, and the gate slowly narrows. One mistake ends the run: a tap outside the gate, or a comet that gets all the way through it. A catch dead in the middle is PERFECT: double points, and the bushes, trees and windows flash gold. The roof stays clear for the comet and the gate: a catch makes only a small gold flash where the gate was, never drawn over the comet or its tail (which is wiped as it turns, so the turn is crisp). Every 10 catches the streak flies into the next bush, which lights up in its own color (the next one fills faintly as the catches add up); when every bush is lit, they flash gold and start over in new colors. The trees are a speedometer, and the windows catch the color of every catch and glow warmer as the speed climbs.
  - **Bead Rush** — a chain of colored beads creeps along the roof toward the launcher at its start. Pick the launcher's color with the left and right buttons (or the arrow keys): the colors sit on a wheel in rainbow order and each press turns it one step, wrapping round. The middle button (or the space bar) fires the loaded bead into the front of the chain at once. Three or more of one color at the front pop and the chain recoils; a shot that does not pop just lengthens the chain toward you. On the phone, the three buttons show the colors they stand for, and the bushes mirror them: the middle bush glows the loaded color, the bushes beside it the colors left and right would pick. Shots that pop in a row build a combo: more points, a harder kick back, and gold sparkle in the other bushes. Gold beads come down the chain after a while: hit one at the front and it blows up the six beads behind it. Stages bring more colors, so the wheel takes more turning: green, blue and magenta at first, cyan after a minute, orange after two. The trees are a danger meter that fills as the chain nears and blinks red when it is close. If the chain reaches you, its front bursts red, it is shoved back and you lose one of three lives. The chain speeds up over the first three minutes, then holds a pace that a player who turns the short way round and moves every 0.4 seconds can keep up with for ever.
  - **Beat Drop** — a rhythm game that never ends. Its game window is an arcade cabinet: the top bar has a score that counts up with each hit's points floating off it, a pop for every judgment, the best score (NEW BEST! once it is beaten) and the multiplier in its color (cyan ×2, magenta ×3, a rainbow ×4); the bottom bar has the meter charging toward the next multiplier, the combo, the stage and the health. A new multiplier is announced in the top bar, the cabinet's edge flashes in its color and the phone buzzes; losing one shakes the top bar and flashes the edge red. Notes in green, cyan, blue and magenta slide along the roof toward the hit zone near its start (a lit band between two bright posts, a couple of feet in from the end of the roof; the posts are drawn over everything, with a dark gap either side, so they stay easy to see through the explosions of a hit), and the phone has a button in each color (on a computer: the arrow keys ← ↓ ↑ →, or D F J K). Press the button in a note's color as its head passes through the zone: the band is the PERFECT window, so it widens as the notes speed up. Past the zone the roof is a faint red, the late side: a note that slides on into it shows you were late. Long notes are holds: press as the head arrives, keep that button down while the body slides in, and let go as the bright end cap reaches the zone (holding past the end counts too). The wrong color counts as a stray, but the note can still be hit with the right one. Notes never come two at once, and a color only changes when there is time to move a finger. The colors come in gently: first just the two outside buttons, then all four in pairs and alternations, then runs up and down the colors, then jumps. Presses are judged by the moment the player saw (Wi-Fi delay is allowed for): PERFECT is worth 2, GOOD 1 (the phone says whether it was early or late), and a hold held to its end earns a bonus by its length, all multiplied by up to ×4, one step for every 8 hits in a row. A miss flashes the zone red, sends red ripples out along the whole roofline and makes the backdrop stutter, breaks the streak and drains the health meter; hits fill it back; empty is game over. Before a game, pick **1 color** (the original one-button game: white notes, the big button), **2 colors** (green and magenta only, with two buttons, ← and →, or F and J) or **4 colors**, and **Easy** (75 to 130 beats a minute, simpler patterns, gentler colors, wider timing), **Normal** (90 to 170) or **Hard** (100 to 176, busier sooner, tighter timing); each combination keeps its own best score. **Stages**: every 8 bars the whole roofline changes its look, sweeping in from the zone to the far end and announced on the phone: Midnight (a calm indigo that breathes on the beat), Ember Tide (warm waves), Aurora (teal and violet curtains), Starfall (twinkling stars), Pulse (ripples on every beat and a sweeping timeline) and Prism (a rainbow that turns on the beat), then round again, stronger each round (gentler on Easy, stronger on Hard). The busier backdrops make the notes harder to pick out, which is part of the difficulty, but the backdrop always gives way under and around every note and in the zone, so the notes stay readable. A PERFECT sends a soft gold ripple along the roof. A new multiplier is celebrated off the roof, so the next notes stay easy to see: every bush flashes (brighter at every step), a gold band runs up the trees and the house flashes, while the roof only gets a soft glow from the zone and a small burst in it; losing a multiplier scatters red sparks along the roof; low health beats red through the roof like a heart. The bushes show the multiplier, one more lit gold for each step, pulsing on the beat and flashing the color of each hit; the trees are the health meter, green when healthy and red and blinking when low; windows and doors bounce on every beat in the stage's color and flare in the colors of the hits.
  - **Whack-a-Mole** — the arcade game, on the whole yard. Every bush and tree is a hole, each in its own color (never red). A mole pops up in one: the bush fills with its color from the ground up, under a white, twinkling head. The phone shows the same hole lit, with a cartoon mole in it: tap it before it ducks back down (a tap while it sinks still counts, for Wi-Fi). On the phone, the holes sit on a grassy deck in a candy-striped frame, at least about 2 cm across for small fingers, in staggered rows that keep the yard's left-to-right order; the number keys whack them on a computer. A whack blows the mole up in color: the bush swirls through the rainbow, a firework bursts over it, a shockwave runs through the house, sparks fly along the roof, the windows catch its color, and the phone pops BONK!, the points and confetti. A mole that gets away flashes red three times and is gone. A round lasts 45 seconds after a 3-2-1 and speeds up as it goes, with more moles up at once. Five whacks in a row double the points, ten triple them and twenty quadruple them; a miss or a whack at an empty hole starts the count over. Now and then a golden mole, in rainbow bands, is worth five times as much. The roof is the clock, an arcade marquee of chasing rainbow lights that shrinks toward the middle of the house, orange and ticking in the last ten seconds. The phone shows the yard as an arcade cabinet with chasing bulbs, the score, the clock and the multiplier. Three speeds (Easy, Normal, Hard), each with its own high-score table. A yard with fewer than three bushes and trees plays on stretches of the roof instead.

A *scene* is what v1 called a *public preset*. A *WLED preset* is a preset stored on one device.

## Run with Docker

```bash
docker run -d \
  --name wled-presets \
  --network host \
  -e ADMIN_USERNAME=admin \
  -e ADMIN_PASSWORD=change-me-to-something-long \
  -e DISCOVERY_ON_STARTUP=1 \
  -e DISCOVERY_AUTO_SAVE=1 \
  -v /path/to/appdata:/app/data \
  ghcr.io/benjithompson/wled-presets:latest
```

Or use [docker-compose.yml](docker-compose.yml). Then open `http://<host>:8790`.

Host networking lets discovery scan your LAN. Without it, add devices by IP address.

### Environment variables

| Variable | Default | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `ADMIN_USERNAME` / `ADMIN_PASSWORD` | — | Creates the first admin account when none exists. Ignored after that. |
| `PORT` | `8790` | Web port. |
| `DATABASE_PATH` | `/app/data/wled-presets.sqlite` | SQLite file. |
| `PUID` / `PGID` | `99` / `100` | Owner of the data folder. The server runs as this user. |
| `DISCOVERY_ON_STARTUP` | `0` | Scan for devices when the container starts. |
| `DISCOVERY_AUTO_SAVE` | `0` | Add devices found by the startup scan. |
| `DISCOVERY_SUBNETS` | auto | Comma-separated CIDR ranges to scan. Overrides the subnets in Settings. |
| `DISCOVERY_DEBUG` | `0` | Log discovery details. |
| `DISCOVERY_HOSTS` | — | Extra hosts to probe on every scan, comma-separated. |
| `DISCOVERY_PORTS` | `80` | Ports to probe on each address, for example `80,8080` or `9101-9104`. |
| `STATUS_INTERVAL_MS` | `10000` | How often device status is checked. |
| `SECURE_COOKIE` | `0` | Set to `1` when you reach the app only over HTTPS. Cookies are also `Secure` on any request that a proxy in front marks as HTTPS (`X-Forwarded-Proto: https`). |
| `TRUST_PROXY` | `0` | Who the visitor is, behind a proxy. `cloudflare` for Cloudflare Tunnel (reads `CF-Connecting-IP`); `1` for one reverse proxy such as nginx (reads the last `X-Forwarded-For` entry; `2` for two proxies, and so on). Without it, every visitor through the proxy shares one IP and one rate limit, and the server log warns about it. |

With no admin account and no environment variables, open `/admin` from the server itself (`http://localhost:8790/admin`) to create one. A request through a proxy or tunnel never counts as "from the server itself".

### On the internet

The public page is safe to share with a forwarded port, a reverse proxy, or Cloudflare Tunnel:

- **Port forwarded straight to the app** (nothing in front): leave `TRUST_PROXY` off. The server sees each visitor's real address. Turning it on here would let visitors make up their address in a header.
- **Behind a reverse proxy or tunnel**: set `TRUST_PROXY=1` (nginx, Caddy, Nginx Proxy Manager) or `TRUST_PROXY=cloudflare`, so rate limits, the line and failed sign-ins are counted per visitor. Keep the app's own port closed to the internet, so requests can only come through the proxy.
- Pick your own `ADMIN_PASSWORD`. The example password in this README creates no account, and an account that still has it only signs in from the home network.
- Failed sign-ins slow down: after 5 from one address, or 10 for one username, each try waits twice as long as the last (up to 15 minutes). Signing in from the home network directly is never locked out by strangers guessing the username.
- Changing the password signs out every other session.
- **Passkeys need HTTPS and the site's name** (`https://lights.example.com`, not `http://203.0.113.9:8790`): browsers only make them there, or on `localhost`. A passkey belongs to the name it was made at, so one made at the home-network name does not sign in at the public name; add one at each. Adding a passkey asks for the password, so a stolen session cookie alone cannot add one. Failed passkey sign-ins count against the address like wrong passwords.

## Upgrade from v1

Point v2 at the same data folder. It reads the v1 database as it is: devices, public presets (now scenes), imported presets, the default preset and the auto-revert time all carry over. The public page keeps its "Christmas Lights" title; change it in Settings.

What changes:

- Admins sign in once more, because sessions are now stored in the main database. `SESSION_SECRET` is no longer used.
- Cookies are no longer `Secure` by default. Set `SECURE_COOKIE=1` if you use HTTPS.
- v1 backup files still import. v2 backups also store device addresses, so a backup restores onto a new install.

## Development

Requires Node.js 24 or newer.

To start everything with one command:

```bash
./run.sh
```

The script installs dependencies and creates `.env` when they are missing. Then it starts four fake WLED devices, the API, and the web client. It also sets `DISCOVERY_HOSTS` and `DISCOVERY_PORTS`, so **Discover** finds the fake devices. Open http://localhost:5790. Press Ctrl+C to stop all three.

To run the parts one at a time:

| Command | What it does |
|---|---|
| `npm run dev:all` | Same as `./run.sh`, without the setup steps. |
| `npm run mock` | Starts four fake WLED devices on 127.0.0.1:9101–9104. Run it in its own terminal. |
| `npm run dev` | Starts the API on port 8790 and the web client with hot reload on port 5790. Open http://localhost:5790. Set `WEB_PORT` or `PORT` in `.env` to change the ports. |
| `npm run check` | Runs the typecheck, the tests and the production build. |

| Part | Stack | Folder |
|---|---|---|
| Server | Node.js 24 (runs TypeScript directly), Hono, better-sqlite3, Zod | [server/src](server/src) |
| Web client | React 19, Vite, Tailwind CSS 4, TanStack Query, React Router | [web/src](web/src) |
| Shared types | TypeScript | [shared/types.ts](shared/types.ts) |
| Tests | Vitest, against fake WLED devices | [server/test](server/test) |

Live updates use server-sent events: the server checks every device on a timer and pushes changes to open pages.

Frames reach the lights through one pipeline ([server/src/framePipeline.ts](server/src/framePipeline.ts)). Everything that draws is a source: the game host (games and scenes, [server/src/game/host.ts](server/src/game/host.ts)) and the editors' preview ([server/src/layoutPreview.ts](server/src/layoutPreview.ts)). One clock ticks every running source 60 times a second, on a steady beat, and the frame of the most important source goes out: an editor over a game, a game over a scene. The pipeline maps each frame to the devices' LED numbers and hands it to the sender ([server/src/lightSender.ts](server/src/lightSender.ts)), which streams DDP over UDP (or HTTP when UDP does not get through), skips a frame the device already shows, and sends each device only as many frames as its strip can show (a 1,000-LED WS2812B output takes 30 ms per frame; the outputs and LED types are read from the device's settings, and outputs side by side count once). A device that no longer gets frames goes back to its own state at once; one that passes from one source to another just keeps going. The Play page's simulated yard goes to each phone 30 times a second, only when it changed, and compressed (deflate): mostly dark game frames shrink 5 to 25 times, so the phones leave the Wi-Fi to the lights. Games draw with the shared kit ([server/src/game/kit.ts](server/src/game/kit.ts)): the roof as seen from the street, sparks, waves, trails and motes that fly through the yard.

## Release

Bump `version` in `package.json` in the pull request. When it is merged into `main`, the workflow in [.github/workflows](.github/workflows) runs the checks, tags the merge `vX.Y.Z`, makes a GitHub release with notes, and publishes an amd64 image to GHCR (`latest`, `X.Y.Z`, `X.Y`, `X`). A merge that keeps the version releases nothing. Pushing a tag such as `v2.0.0` by hand still works too.
